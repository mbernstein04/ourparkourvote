What Happened

Hundreds of Valid Signatures, Left Out of the Count

Under the City Charter, the Clerk's office must examine every petition sheet and count the signatures of qualified Duluth voters. On the 230-acre petition, hundreds of those voters were never counted — for two reasons, and neither is that anyone questioned whether the signers were real, registered residents.

Roughly 165 sheets were never counted at all

To validate signatures, the Clerk's office worked from photocopies of the petition sheets. About 165 of the original sheets were never copied — so they were never reviewed and never counted. Every qualified voter who signed them was simply left out of the total.

53 more were excluded over two lines of boilerplate

Another 53 sheets were set aside — not because the signers weren't qualified, but because those sheets didn't carry two lines of printed text: a "subject to public inspection" notice and a disability-related instruction drawn from a state rule. The signatures on them are genuine signatures of registered Duluth voters.

The Clerk had already found our form compliant

Here is what makes the exclusion hard to justify: before those sheets were ever circulated, the Clerk's office reviewed our petition form and confirmed in writing that it "appears to be compliant" — and it did so after telling us, two weeks earlier, that the form would be reviewed by the City Attorney's office. The suggestion to add that language came afterward — offered as the Clerk's own "recommendation," and only months later described as something the rule "will require." Sheets circulated in good-faith reliance on a form the City itself reviewed and approved should not be nullified for missing words the office never asked for.

The result: more than 4,000 eligible Duluthians signed to put this on the ballot, and hundreds were left uncounted over paperwork the City had already blessed. See what the lawsuit asks the court to do →

Our Demand

We Counted On the City to Count Every Signature.
It Didn't.

Duluthians signed two voter-initiative petitions to put the future of the former Lester Park Golf Course to a public vote. Under the City Charter, the Clerk's office must examine every petition sheet. On the 230-acre petition, it didn't: roughly 165 signature sheets were never counted at all, and 53 more were wrongly rejected — leaving the petition certified below the 20% threshold that would put the measure on the November 2026 ballot.

So we went to court. The demand is simple: finish the count. Review the sheets that were skipped, certify both petitions under the correct standard, and let this land's future be decided the way it should be — by the people who live here, this November.

The Lawsuit, in Brief

The caseBernstein v. Denham — a Verified Petition for a Writ of Mandamus and Declaratory Relief, filed July 15, 2026 in St. Louis County District Court (No. 69DU-CV-26-1924), on an expedited basis.
Who filed itMichael Bernstein — a registered Duluth voter, Director of Our Park, Our Vote, and the proposer of both petitions — on his own behalf.
Filed againstCity Clerk Alyssa Denham (in her official capacity), the Duluth City Council, and the City of Duluth.
What happenedTwo voter petitions sought to send the future of the former Lester Park Golf Course to a public vote. On the 230-acre petition, the Clerk left roughly 165 signature sheets uncounted and wrongly rejected 53 more — certifying it below the 20% threshold. The 37.5-acre "Lake 9" petition already had enough valid signatures but was processed under the wrong standard. Either error would push the vote from November 2026 to November 2027 — after the City's planned rezoning could already be done.
What it asks the courtOrder the Clerk to finish the count and certify both petitions correctly, order the Council to act within the Charter's deadlines so the measures reach the November 3, 2026 ballot, and bar any rezoning or sale while the case is pending.
What's at stakeMore than 4,000 eligible Duluthians — including over 1,100 whose signatures were never counted — could lose their chance to vote on this land this cycle.
The ask is very simple — just demanding that the Clerk's office finish counting those sheets. — Duluth City Councilor Wendy Durrwachter
