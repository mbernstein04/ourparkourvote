What the Petitions Actually Do

The voters decide.

The petitions put all of Lester Park under park zoning — and let Duluth voters decide, by majority vote, whether any of it is ever rezoned for development.

What the petitions actually do

Two petitions, one principle: the voters decide.

Petition 1 — the upper 230 acres.

This land is zoned parkland today. Petition 1 does not change its zoning — it adds one requirement: before the City rezones this parkland for private development, a majority of Duluth voters must approve. Nothing is "reclaimed," because nothing was ever lost. The petition simply says the public's land doesn't change hands without the public's consent.

Petition 2 — the lower 37.5 acres.

This parcel was rezoned to mixed use years ago for a development that never happened: the developer's option lapsed, no permit was ever issued, nothing was built, and the land sits vacant in public hands. Petition 2 returns it to park zoning — through the City's own standard rezoning process, with the planning commission review, public notice, and hearings that process requires — and applies the same rule going forward: rezoning for development requires the voters' approval.

What the petitions do NOT do.

They do not prohibit housing at Lester Park. They do not touch any permit, variance, or site-plan approval anywhere in Duluth. They do not "overturn" any zoning decision — the upper parcel was never rezoned, and the lower parcel's rezoning is changed prospectively through the ordinary process, exactly as the Council itself could do at any meeting. They decide one thing only: who gives the final approval when public parkland is turned over to private development. We think that should be the public.

We are not against housing. We're for getting it where it works.

Duluth needs housing — the City's own 2025 study says nearly 8,700 units by 2035, much of it affordable to middle- and low-income individuals and families in Duluth. We support that goal. Our concern about Lester Park is practical: it sits on bedrock, with no infrastructure in place. Duluth is full of sites that already have buildable ground and services — downtown, the mall corridor, and parcels like Lot D that are already zoned and served — where the same public dollar buys far more housing Duluthians can actually afford.

And if housing at Lester Park is ultimately determined to make sense, a public vote on rezoning is not an obstacle. It's leverage. A developer and a council that need majority approval will come to the voters with real, funded affordability commitments — not requirements on paper with no money attached, which is what Lester Park got last time: in 2021–22 the City required at least 80 units of deeply affordable housing on the lower parcel and attached no funding of any kind. The requirement failed because it was never financed, not because housing is impossible. And a development deal is never guaranteed to happen at all: at Incline Village, the planned project's agreement with the City ended and it is no longer moving forward. Trading permanent parkland for a development that may never materialize is a poor bargain — a public vote simply keeps that trade-off in the public's hands.

The vote is how the public makes sure that losing parkland is a choice Duluth makes — and a deal worth taking.

We're not fighting housing. We're fighting the wrong use of public land.

Both halves of that sentence deserve to be said plainly.

The part of Lester Park that can be responsibly developed should be considered for housing that meets Duluth's actual needs — homes working families can afford, at the depth of affordability the City's own studies say we're short of. If the City and a developer bring the voters a plan like that, we expect Duluthians to say yes. A public vote isn't a wall. It's a bar, and good projects clear it.

What we oppose is the other thing on the table: reviving a golf course on public land. Duluth has already run that experiment — the City stopped operating the Lester Park course because it lost money. Bringing it back means committing public land and public money to a use that is financially and environmentally unsustainable: a course that operates at a loss, spreads fertilizer and pesticides above streams that run to Lake Superior, and keeps 200 football fields' worth of public land in mowed turf for the few instead of habitat, trails, and open parkland for everyone. Public land should not be anyone's subsidized private playground. Whoever wants to build one can buy their own land.

That is the choice the petitions protect: before public parkland is committed, the public decides whether the trade is worth it.

Frequently asked questions

Is this a referendum?

No. These are initiative petitions under City Charter § 51 — proposals for new ordinances, a power Duluth's Charter has given its citizens since 1912. A referendum undoes a recently passed ordinance; an initiative proposes a new one. Nothing here undoes anything.

Does this ban housing at Lester Park?

No. Either parcel can be developed for housing — if a majority of Duluth voters approve the rezoning. The petitions decide who decides, not what gets built.

Doesn't this "overturn" zoning decisions?

No. The upper 230 acres were never rezoned — they are parkland today. The lower 37.5 acres would return to park zoning through the City's own standard rezoning process, the same way the Council changes zoning routinely.

Are you against all development at Lester Park?

No. The portion that can be responsibly developed should be considered for housing that meets Duluth's actual needs, and a plan that does that can win a public vote. What we oppose is committing public land to reviving a money-losing golf course — a financially and environmentally unsustainable use that serves the few at everyone's expense.

Would this open the floodgates to zoning by petition?

Duluth has had this power since 1912. In 114 years it has been used on land use four times. Reaching the ballot takes signatures from 20% of the city's last-election voters; passing takes a citywide majority. Nobody runs that gauntlet over a routine rezoning.

Where do things stand now?

Both petitions were certified as sufficient — more than 4,000 valid signatures each, against a threshold of 3,683. On July 27, 2026, the City Council voted 7–2 not to place them on the ballot. The question of whether the measures go before voters on November 3 is now before the St. Louis County District Court. Ballot questions must be certified to the County by August 11.
